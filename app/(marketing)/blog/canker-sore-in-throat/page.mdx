A canker sore is a local problem: one spot of lining breaks down, it hurts, it
closes. Almost everything else that makes the back of your mouth sore is not
local — it is an illness that happens to include your throat. That is the whole
distinction this page is about, and it is usually decidable without anybody
looking down your throat at all.

## Can you get a canker sore in your throat?

Yes, but be precise about where. The back of the mouth is not one surface. The
soft palate, and the arches of tissue running down either side of the tonsil,
are the soft, movable, non-keratinised lining that canker sores favour
everywhere else in the mouth — the same kind of tissue as the inside of your
lip. A genuine aphthous ulcer there is unremarkable: round, shallow, a pale
floor and a red rim, painful out of proportion to its size because you swallow
about a thousand times a day.

The tonsil itself is not that tissue. It is lymphoid tissue with deep pits in
its surface, and the things that commonly happen to it are infections and
debris rather than ulcers. So "a canker sore on my tonsil" is a reasonable
thing to type into a search box, and the honest answer is that a sore _on_ the
tonsil is more often something else.

## Is a white spot on my tonsil a canker sore?

Usually not, and the useful test is coating versus crater.

An **ulcer** is a break in the surface. It has an edge you could trace, the
floor sits lower than the tissue around it, and nothing about it lifts off.

A **coating** sits on top of intact tissue — the white or yellow film of a
throat infection, spread across the tonsil or in patches on both sides. A
**tonsil stone** is a firm white or yellow lump packed into one of the tonsil's
pits, often with bad breath and often with no pain at all; they can sometimes be
dislodged, which an ulcer cannot.

If what you can see wipes, lifts, or is on both sides, the word "ulcer" is
probably the wrong word for it, and this page is probably the wrong page.

## Is it a canker sore or tonsillitis?

Fever is the line worth trusting, and it is the reason this page leads with the
distinction between a local sore and an illness.

A canker sore does not cause a fever. It does not cause swollen glands in your
neck, it does not make you feel generally unwell, and it does not make the whole
of swallowing hurt — it makes one identifiable spot hurt, more when something
touches it. If you can point to where it is, and you feel otherwise fine, a
local sore is a reasonable thing to think you have.

A throat infection is the other shape entirely: sore on both sides, worse over a
day or two, usually with a fever and tender glands under the jaw, and often with
nothing you can point at because the whole throat is the problem. That is not a
sore to measure. That is an illness to have looked at if it is not settling.

## What else puts sores at the back of the mouth

One sentence each, because the point is not to work out which of these it is —
it is to know that the list is long enough that guessing is a poor use of your
afternoon.

- **A viral sore throat**, far and away the most common: sore on both sides,
  often with a cold, and better within a week.
- **Bacterial throat infection**, which tends to come with a fever, tender neck
  glands and a coated tonsil, and without the cough and runny nose of a viral
  one.
- **Herpangina and hand-foot-and-mouth**, which put small ulcers across the soft
  palate and the back of the mouth, mostly in children, with a fever, and
  sometimes a rash on the hands and feet.
- **Glandular fever**, which is a severe sore throat with very swollen tonsils
  and glands and exhaustion that is out of proportion to everything else,
  typically in teenagers and young adults.
- **A first herpes infection**, which puts many ulcers across the mouth and gums
  at once along with a fever, and which is [not what a single ordinary canker
  sore is](/blog/are-canker-sores-herpes).
- **Something you scratched**, which is a real and underrated answer at the
  back of the mouth: a crisp, a crust of toast, a fishbone. It hurts sharply
  from the moment it happens and it is better in a few days.
- **A tonsil stone**, which is debris rather than a sore, and is the thing most
  often mistaken for an ulcer on a tonsil.

There is also the case where recurring mouth ulcers are part of something wider
— ulcers elsewhere as well as in the mouth, or with joint, eye or skin symptoms.
That is not diagnosable from a page and is not something to track your way
through; it is a reason to bring the whole pattern to a doctor.

## How long should it take?

If it is a canker sore, the same course as anywhere else: worst pain in the
first two to three days, widest around the middle of the week, closing from the
edges inward, and [gone within 7 to 14
days](/blog/how-long-do-canker-sores-last). The back of the mouth does not have
its own timetable.

Anything at the back of the mouth that has not healed within three weeks should
be looked at, regardless of what it looks like, how small it is, or how little
it hurts by then. That is not a prediction about what it is. It is the
threshold at which waiting stops being a reasonable plan.

## When to stop guessing

**This week**, if any of these are true:

- a fever, or tender swollen glands in your neck
- pain on swallowing rather than pain when something touches one spot
- pain on one side that has lasted more than a few days
- a sore at the back of the mouth that is not clearly better after a fortnight
- repeated bouts of the same thing

## If it does turn out to be an ordinary one

Then it has a course, and the only thing today's appearance tells you is where
in that course you are — which is nothing, until there is a second look to
compare it with. A sore at the back of the mouth is the hardest place to measure
honestly, so if you are going to track it, track it with a phone light and a
mirror at the same time of day, and accept that the number is rougher here than
it would be on a lip.

[Whether it is healing](/blog/is-my-canker-sore-healing) is still a question
about millimetres. It is just a question about worse millimetres.
