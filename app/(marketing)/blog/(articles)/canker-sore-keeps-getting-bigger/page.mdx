This is the day-two panic, and it is almost always a false alarm. A canker
sore does not appear at full size and shrink from there. It opens small,
widens for two or three days, stops, and only then starts closing — so the
stretch where it is visibly getting worse is a normal part of the course
rather than a departure from it.

The useful question is not whether it grew. It is **when** it grew.

## Is it normal for a canker sore to get bigger?

In the first three days, yes. That is the single most common reason people
decide something has gone wrong when nothing has.

The sequence is the same almost every time: a day of tingling before anything
is visible, then a small crater with a white or yellow floor and a red ring,
then two or three days of that crater widening as the surface breaks down
around its edge. Full size lands around day three or four. After that it fills
in from the rim, which means **the white centre shrinks before the sore
disappears** — one more thing that looks like deterioration and is not.

The [day-by-day course](/blog/how-long-do-canker-sores-last) is the long
version, and [the five stages drawn](/blog/canker-sore-stages) is what each
of those steps actually looks like.

## When getting bigger is worth acting on

Three cases, and they are narrow on purpose:

1. **Still widening after about day four.** Ordinary sores stop early. One
   that has not is off the course this page describes.
2. **Past 10mm across.** That is the uncommon major kind — slower, sometimes
   weeks rather than days, and worth showing to a dentist.
3. **Spreading rather than widening.** One round crater getting wider is the
   normal thing. A patch that is extending irregularly, or new ulcers joining
   up with it, is a different observation.

Everything else — that it hurts more, that it looks angrier, that the white
part is bigger than yesterday — is the ordinary first half of an ordinary
sore.

## How big do canker sores get?

| Width at its widest | Usually gone by  | What it is                                                      |
| ------------------- | ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Under 5mm           | Day 7–10         | A minor aphthous ulcer — the ordinary kind                      |
| 5–10mm              | Day 10–14        | Still minor, just wider, so it takes longer to fill in          |
| Over 10mm           | Two weeks to six | A major ulcer. Uncommon, slower, and worth showing to a dentist |

Width is the best single predictor of how long yours will take, which is why
it is the number worth having and pain is not. A crater fills in from its rim,
so a 10mm sore is a good deal more than twice the work of a 5mm one.

## Why it feels bigger than it looks

Because pain tracks _where_ a sore is far more than how wide it is. A 3mm
ulcer sitting exactly where your teeth close can be agonising; a broad shallow
one on the inside of a cheek can be merely annoying and take twice as long.

Pain also peaks in the first two to three days and fades from day four onward,
while the sore itself is clearly visible for another week. So early on, rising
pain and increasing size happen together and feel like one event — and then
they separate, and the sore keeps closing long after it has stopped hurting.
They are two measurements that do not move together, which is why the app
records them separately instead of asking how it is going.

## The only way to know whether it is still growing

Eyeballing a sore against a ruler is good to about a millimetre. That sets the
whole method:

1. Measure the widest point in millimetres, in decent light.
2. Write it down with the date.
3. Measure again two days later.

One millimetre of difference is noise. Two is real. And a single look — today,
without yesterday's number — cannot tell you whether it is growing, stalled or
closing, no matter how carefully you look at it.
