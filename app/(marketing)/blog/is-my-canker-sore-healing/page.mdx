Almost every answer to this question is a list of things to look for, which is
useless in practice, because all of them are gradual. A sore does not stop
looking like an angry hole one morning. It narrows by half a millimetre a day,
which is invisible to someone checking it in a bathroom mirror and obvious to
anyone who wrote a number down.

So this page is in two halves: the signs, which are real but weak, and the
test, which actually works.

## How do you know when a canker sore is healing?

Four things change, roughly in this order.

**The pain drops first.** Usually from around day four. This is the least
reliable signal of the four, because it moves well ahead of the sore itself —
people regularly conclude a sore has healed on the day it stopped hurting and
then find it still there a week later.

**The red ring fades.** The inflamed halo around the crater goes from angry
red to a duller pink. This is a genuine sign and easier to see than size,
because colour does not need a ruler.

**The white centre shrinks.** A canker sore heals from the edges inward, so
the pale floor gets smaller while the overall spot is still visible. A sore
that is mostly pink with a small white middle is further along than one that
is mostly white.

**The whole thing gets narrower.** The one that actually settles it, and the
one nobody can judge by eye from memory.

## Do canker sores get worse before they get better?

Yes, and this is the part most people get wrong. A canker sore normally widens
and hurts more for the first two to three days. Then it holds roughly still
for a few days before it starts closing.

That plateau is where this question gets asked. Nothing is visibly improving,
the pain has dulled but not gone, and it has been the same for three days —
which reads as stuck, and is usually just the middle of a perfectly ordinary
course. Getting worse early is expected. Getting worse after day four is not.

## The test that actually works

Measure the width at its widest point, in millimetres, write it down with the
date, and do it again two days later.

That is the whole method. It works because it replaces a judgement your memory
is bad at — _is this smaller than it was?_ — with a comparison between two
numbers, which is a judgement nobody can get wrong.

Two details make the difference between a measurement and a guess:

- **Same light, same angle, every time.** A sore looks bigger under a
  bathroom light than by a window.
- **Widest point across, not the length of the sore end to end.** Consistency
  with yourself matters more than which convention you pick.

## What a healing canker sore looks like

Smaller, flatter, paler, and less painful to touch — in that order of
reliability, reading right to left. If you want one thing to look for rather
than four, look at how much of the spot is still white: a crater filling in
from its edges is the clearest visual sign that the sore is closing, and
unlike size it is a change you can actually see between two days.

## Signs it is not healing

- **Still widening after day four.**
- **No measurable change by day fourteen.** Not still visible — still the same
  width. Most sores are still visible at two weeks; few are still the same
  size.
- **Still there at three weeks**, at any size.
- **A new one appearing in the same place** each time the last one clears.
  That is [a pattern rather than a
  sore](/blog/why-do-i-keep-getting-canker-sores), and it is usually worth
  looking at what is at that spot.
- **Fever, a rash, or sores elsewhere on the body** alongside it.

The first three are reasons to have a dentist look. The last two are worth
mentioning to a doctor, because recurring ulcers in a fixed spot and ulcers
with symptoms elsewhere are the cases where the mouth is not the whole story.

## Why you cannot tell by looking

A minor canker sore shrinks by something like half a millimetre a day. Over
two days that is one millimetre — real, measurable progress, and completely
invisible to someone comparing today's mouth against a mental image of
Tuesday's.

This is not a failure of attention. Nobody can hold a millimetre in their
head. It is the reason [Canker Core](/) exists as a log rather than a
checklist: two numbers and a date, taken twice, answer the question that no
amount of staring in a mirror can.
