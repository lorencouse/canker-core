The reason this question gets asked so often is that a canker sore gives you
almost no feedback. It hurts about the same on day two as it does on day five,
you cannot see it properly without a phone torch and an awkward angle, and
every day it is still there feels like evidence that something is wrong. So
here is the whole course, laid out in days.

## How long does a canker sore last?

For most people, **seven to fourteen days**, and the width at its worst is
what decides where in that range you land.

| Width at its widest | Usually gone by  | What it is                                                      |
| ------------------- | ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Under 5mm           | Day 7–10         | A minor aphthous ulcer — the ordinary kind                      |
| 5–10mm              | Day 10–14        | Still minor, just wider, so it takes longer to fill in          |
| Over 10mm           | Two weeks to six | A major ulcer. Uncommon, slower, and worth showing to a dentist |

Width matters more than pain here. A 3mm sore in a spot your tooth keeps
catching can be agonising and still be gone by Friday; a broad, shallow one on
the inside of a cheek can be merely annoying and take a fortnight.

## The day-by-day course

**Day 0–1, before you can see it.** A patch of mouth tingles, prickles or
burns. Nothing is visible yet. This is the stage where people say they can
feel one coming, and they are usually right.

**Day 1–3, it opens and grows.** A small round crater appears with a white or
yellow floor and a red ring around it. It gets wider over these days, and this
is when it hurts most. **A canker sore getting bigger in the first three days
is normal.** It is the single most common reason people decide something has
gone wrong when nothing has.

**Day 4–7, the standstill.** The growing stops. The pain starts dropping away
noticeably, often faster than the sore itself changes. The white centre is
still there and the sore looks, to the naked eye, more or less identical from
one day to the next. This is the stretch that feels endless, and it is also
the stretch where measuring is the only thing that will tell you anything.

**Day 7–14, it closes.** The crater fills in from the edges, so the white
centre shrinks before the sore vanishes. The red halo fades to pink, then to
nothing. A minor ulcer heals without scarring — if you can still find the spot
a month later by looking, that is worth mentioning to a dentist.

## How long does canker sore pain last?

Less time than the sore does. Pain typically peaks in the first two to three
days and eases from day four onward, while the sore itself is still clearly
there for another week.

That gap trips people up in both directions. Feeling better on day five does
not mean it has healed, and still being able to see it on day ten after the
pain has gone does not mean it is stuck. Pain and size are two different
measurements and they do not move together — which is exactly why
[Canker Core](/) records them separately rather than asking you how it is
going.

## What makes one last longer

- **Where it is.** Anywhere that moves or gets caught — the inside of a lip,
  the edge of the tongue, the line your teeth close on — gets re-irritated
  daily, and a wound that is disturbed every day heals slowly.
- **How wide it got.** The single best predictor. Filling in a 10mm crater is
  more work than filling in a 3mm one.
- **Whether you keep touching it.** Prodding a sore with your tongue is almost
  involuntary and it genuinely extends the course.
- **Having several at once.** Not slower individually, but the overlap means
  the mouth feels continuously sore for weeks, which is usually what people
  mean when they say one has lasted a month.

## When has it lasted too long?

Three practical lines, none of which are a diagnosis:

1. **Still growing after day four.** Ordinary sores stop widening early.
2. **No visible narrowing by day fourteen.** Not "still there" — still the
   same size. That is the distinction that matters, and it is the one you
   cannot make from memory.
3. **Past three weeks at all**, or wider than 10mm, or recurring in the same
   spot every few weeks.

Any of those is worth a dentist. Not because it is likely to be something
serious, but because at that point the useful thing is an examination rather
than another week of waiting.

## How to know, instead of guessing

Everything above is a population range. It tells you what canker sores do in
general, which is genuinely useful on day three and almost useless on day
nine, when the only question you care about is whether _yours_ is closing.

There is one reliable way to answer that, and it takes about fifteen seconds:

1. Measure the width across the widest part, in millimetres, in decent light.
2. Write it down with the date.
3. Do it again two days later and compare the two numbers.

A sore that has gone from 6mm to 4mm is healing, whatever it feels like. A
sore that reads 6mm both times has not moved, whatever you hoped. Memory is
not up to this job — a sore you looked at on Tuesday and again on Thursday
will look the same to you both times, because two millimetres is invisible to
recollection and obvious to a ruler.
