People often use "canker sore" and "cold sore" interchangeably, but they are different conditions with different causes, different locations and different implications for the people around you. The quickest way to tell them apart is to ask one question: **is it inside the mouth or on the outside?**

## Canker sores: inside the mouth

A canker sore, or aphthous ulcer, is an open sore on the soft tissue inside the mouth: the inner lips and cheeks, the underside and sides of the tongue, the floor of the mouth, the soft palate, or the base of the gums. It is round or oval, shallow, with a white, yellow or grey centre and a red border.

Canker sores are **not caused by an infection**, and they are **not contagious**. You cannot pass one to another person by kissing, sharing a glass or sharing a fork. The exact cause is not fully understood. It appears to involve an immune response in the lining of the mouth, and it is influenced by things like minor injury, stress, sleep, certain foods, hormonal changes and, in some people, nutritional shortfalls. Canker sores often run in families.

They do not form blisters first, and they do not crust over. They simply appear, hurt for a few days, and heal in one to two weeks.

## Cold sores: on or around the lips

A cold sore, also called a fever blister, is caused by the **herpes simplex virus**, most often HSV-1. It usually appears on the outside of the mouth: on the lip, at the corner of the mouth, or on the skin around it. Less commonly, herpes lesions can appear inside the mouth on firmer tissue such as the gums or hard palate, particularly during a first infection, but the classic cold sore is an outside-the-mouth problem.

Cold sores go through recognisable stages: a tingling or itching spot, then a cluster of small fluid-filled blisters, which break, weep, and finally crust over and heal, usually within about 7 to 10 days. Many people feel the tingle a day before anything is visible.

Cold sores **are contagious**, most of all while blisters are present and weeping. The virus spreads through direct contact, including kissing and sharing items that touch the lips. After the first infection, the virus stays in the body and can reactivate, often triggered by sunlight, illness, fatigue or stress.

## Why the distinction matters

Getting this right changes what you do next. A canker sore can be managed with comfort measures and, if you get them often, a search for your own triggers. A cold sore is a viral infection: antiviral treatments exist and work best when started early, and taking care not to spread it to other people, especially babies, people with eczema and anyone with a weakened immune system, matters.

It also matters for tracking. Canker Core is built for aphthous ulcers, the kind inside the mouth. Its mouth map, factor list and treatment insights are designed around them. If what you get is on the outside of your lip, in a recurring spot, and goes through a blister stage, it is most likely a cold sore and a pharmacist or doctor is the better first stop.

## When to see someone

See a dentist or doctor if:

- you are not sure which kind of sore you have, especially the first time it happens;
- a sore inside the mouth lasts more than two weeks;
- cold sores are frequent, very painful, or spreading beyond the lip;
- you have a weakened immune system, or the sore is near your eye;
- a sore comes with fever, difficulty swallowing, or you feel generally unwell.

A pharmacist can often help with a straightforward cold sore. For anything inside the mouth that is not behaving the way your usual canker sores do, a dentist is a good first call.
