Most canker sores, the small round ulcers that appear inside the mouth, heal on their own in **7 to 14 days**. That range is wide because it covers different kinds of sores and different people. Knowing where a sore sits in that range, and what a normal course looks like for you, is most of what you need to decide whether to wait or to book an appointment.

## The typical timeline

Canker sores (the medical term is recurrent aphthous stomatitis, or aphthous ulcers) tend to follow a predictable arc:

1. **Prodrome, up to a day or two.** Many people notice a tingling, burning or rough patch before anything is visible. This is often the best moment to note what happened in the previous day or two.
2. **Ulcer forms, day 1 to 3.** A shallow, round or oval sore appears with a white, yellow or grey centre and a red rim. Pain usually peaks in the first three or four days, especially when eating, talking or brushing nearby.
3. **Plateau, roughly day 3 to 7.** The sore stops growing. It still hurts, but the sharp edge of the pain usually softens.
4. **Healing, day 7 onward.** The centre shrinks, the rim fades, and eating becomes comfortable again. Minor sores heal without leaving a mark.

If you log size and pain daily, this shape shows up clearly: a quick rise, a flat top, and a longer tail.

## Minor, major and herpetiform

Not all canker sores are the same size, and size is the best everyday predictor of duration.

- **Minor aphthae** are the common kind: usually under 10 mm across, one to a few at a time, healing in about one to two weeks without scarring. Roughly eight in ten people who get canker sores get this type.
- **Major aphthae** are larger (over 10 mm), deeper and more painful. They can take several weeks, sometimes more than a month, to heal, and may leave a small scar. They are much less common and are a reason to see a dentist or doctor rather than wait it out.
- **Herpetiform ulcers** are clusters of many tiny sores that can merge into an irregular patch. Despite the name, they are not caused by the herpes virus. They usually heal in one to two weeks but can be very uncomfortable while they last.

## What prolongs healing

A sore that is dragging on past its usual course often has a reason. Common ones include:

- **Ongoing irritation.** A sore on the cheek that keeps getting bitten, or next to a sharp tooth edge, braces or a rough filling, is re-injured every day.
- **Location.** Sores on the tongue and the floor of the mouth move constantly and tend to feel worse and heal more slowly than sores on the inner lip.
- **Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) toothpaste.** Some people find that switching to an SLS-free toothpaste shortens their sores. The research is mixed, but the trial is cheap.
- **Acidic and abrasive foods.** Citrus, tomatoes, pineapple, crisps and hard bread do not cause an ulcer, but they can keep the surface from settling.
- **Poor sleep, illness and stress.** Anything that puts the body under load appears to slow mouth healing for many people.
- **Smoking cessation.** Some people notice more or longer sores in the weeks after they quit. This usually settles, and it is not a reason to restart.

## Recurrence is part of the picture

For people who get canker sores regularly, the more useful number is often not how long one sore lasts but how long the gap between flare-ups is. Some people get a new sore every few weeks; others a few times a year. Both patterns can be normal, but a sudden change in frequency, or sores that overlap so that you are never free of them, is worth mentioning to a professional.

## When to see someone

Book an appointment with a dentist or doctor if:

- a sore has not healed after **two weeks**;
- a sore is larger than about 10 mm, or unusually deep;
- you have several sores at once, or new ones appear before old ones heal;
- a sore comes with fever, feeling generally unwell, or swollen glands;
- pain is stopping you from eating or drinking normally;
- you also have sores on the skin, eyes or genitals, or ongoing stomach problems.

A sore that lasts longer than three weeks should always be examined, even if it does not hurt.
