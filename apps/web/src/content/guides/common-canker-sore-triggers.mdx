Nobody knows exactly why some people get canker sores and others do not. What is known is that the people who get them tend to have **personal triggers**, things that make a new sore more likely in the following day or two. The list below covers the ones that come up most often in research and in clinics. Not all of them will apply to you, and some may apply only in combination. That is why this guide ends with a note on tracking rather than a rule.

## Mouth injury

The most consistent trigger is minor trauma to the lining of the mouth. Biting the inside of a cheek, a scratch from a crisp or crusty bread, a toothbrush slip, dental work, braces or a rough edge on a tooth can all start a sore a day or two later. If your sores tend to appear in the same place, look for something in that spot that keeps catching the tissue.

## SLS toothpaste

Sodium lauryl sulfate is the foaming agent in most toothpastes. A number of small studies have found fewer or shorter sores in people who switched to an SLS-free paste; others found no difference. The evidence is genuinely mixed. It is a cheap, low-risk change to try for a couple of months, and a good candidate for tracking because you either use it or you do not.

## Acidic, spicy and hard foods

Citrus fruit, tomatoes, pineapple, strawberries, vinegar, coffee and spicy dishes are widely reported as triggers, and they certainly make an existing sore sting. Whether they start new ones is less clear. Chocolate, nuts, cheese and wheat also appear on many people's lists. Rather than cutting everything, note what you ate and let the pattern emerge.

## Stress and sleep

Periods of stress, and the poor sleep that often goes with them, are among the most commonly reported triggers, and several studies have found an association between stress and new outbreaks. The likely mechanism is an effect on the immune response in the mouth's lining. Exams, deadlines, travel and a run of short nights are worth logging as factors even when nothing else changed.

## Hormonal cycles

Some women notice sores that appear at a particular point in their menstrual cycle, often in the days before a period, and that improve during pregnancy or after menopause. This is not universal, and the research is thin, but if you suspect a link, logging your cycle alongside sores is the only way to check.

## Nutritional shortfalls

Low levels of **vitamin B12, iron, folate and zinc** have each been linked to recurrent canker sores in some people, and correcting a genuine deficiency sometimes reduces outbreaks. This does not mean supplements help everyone; if your levels are normal, extra is unlikely to do anything. If you get frequent sores, it is reasonable to ask a doctor about a blood test rather than guess.

## Illness and immune changes

Colds, flu, stomach bugs and other infections often precede a flare-up, presumably because the immune system is busy. Some medicines, including certain anti-inflammatory painkillers and beta blockers, have been associated with mouth ulcers in some people. Never stop a prescribed medicine on your own, but do mention the pattern to whoever prescribed it.

## Quitting smoking

Paradoxically, people who stop smoking sometimes get more canker sores for a few weeks or months. It usually settles. It is worth knowing about so that it does not become a reason to start again.

## Conditions that look like triggers

Occasionally, frequent mouth ulcers are a sign of something else: coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, Behçet's disease, or a weakened immune system. Ulcers that come with stomach symptoms, weight loss, joint pain, eye problems or sores elsewhere on the body need a proper assessment rather than a trigger diary.

## Why the evidence is mixed

Most trigger research relies on people remembering what happened before a sore appeared, and memory is selective. Studies are small, and canker sores probably have several overlapping causes that differ from person to person. The honest summary is: **these factors matter for some people, none of them matters for everyone, and the only way to find yours is to record what happened and look at the numbers.**

## When to see someone

Talk to a dentist or doctor if:

- you get canker sores more than a few times a year, or they are rarely fully gone;
- a sore lasts more than two weeks, or is unusually large;
- you have ulcers alongside stomach problems, weight loss, joint pain or eye symptoms;
- you suspect a medicine is involved;
- you would like to check B12, iron, folate or zinc levels.
