The great majority of mouth ulcers are ordinary canker sores. They hurt, they heal within two weeks, and they do not need a professional. But a small number are something else, and the only way to know is to have them looked at. This guide lists the signs that mean it is time to stop waiting, and explains what to expect when you go.

## The clear signs

**A sore that has lasted more than two weeks.** This is the most important rule. A typical canker sore has healed, or is clearly healing, by day 14. A sore that is unchanged, or still growing, at two weeks should be examined. A sore that has been there for three weeks should be examined even if it does not hurt. Painless, persistent ulcers are the ones most worth taking seriously, because they can be a sign of conditions that need early attention.

**A very large sore.** Ulcers larger than about 10 mm across, or noticeably deep, are called major aphthae. They take much longer to heal, can scar, and sometimes need prescription treatment. They are also more likely than small sores to be something other than a simple canker sore.

**Many sores at once.** A few sores together is common. A mouth full of them, or a constant overlap where new ones appear before old ones heal, is not, and points to something that needs a diagnosis rather than a diary.

**Fever or feeling generally unwell.** Canker sores on their own do not cause fever. An ulcer that arrives with a temperature, swollen glands, a rash or a general feeling of illness is more likely to be an infection or a systemic condition.

**Difficulty eating, drinking or swallowing.** If pain is stopping you from taking in fluids, or you are losing weight because eating hurts, you need help managing it. This is especially urgent in children and older adults, who dehydrate quickly.

**Sores that recur more than a few times a year.** Frequent recurrence is not dangerous in itself, but it is worth a proper assessment. A doctor can check for nutritional deficiencies (B12, iron, folate, zinc), review your medicines, and consider whether an underlying condition such as coeliac disease or inflammatory bowel disease is involved.

## Other reasons to go

- Ulcers alongside sores on the skin, eyes or genitals, or ongoing stomach problems.
- A sore on the tongue or floor of the mouth that feels firm or has raised, rolled edges.
- Any ulcer if you smoke or drink heavily, or have a weakened immune system.
- Bleeding from an ulcer without an obvious cause.
- A white or red patch that does not wipe off and does not go away.
- Numbness in the lip or tongue near the sore.

## Dentist or doctor?

Either is a good starting point. Dentists look inside mouths all day and are very good at spotting an ulcer that does not fit the usual pattern. If a sore comes with symptoms elsewhere in the body, or you suspect a nutritional or medication cause, a doctor is the better first call. Both can refer you to an oral medicine specialist if needed.

## What to expect

The appointment is usually simple. The clinician will look at the sore, ask when it started, how it has changed, whether you have had others, and what else has been going on. They may ask about diet, stress, sleep, medicines, bowel symptoms and family history.

For a sore that is behaving unusually, they may take a swab, order blood tests, or in some cases take a small tissue sample (a biopsy) to rule out anything serious. Most of the time the answer is reassuring, and you leave with a plan: a prescription rinse or paste, a change to try, or a date to come back if it has not healed.

## What to bring

Records make the visit far more useful. Try to be able to say:

- when the current sore started and how it has changed;
- how long your sores usually last and how often they come;
- where in the mouth they tend to appear;
- what you have tried and whether it seemed to help;
- anything that changed in the days before recent sores.

## When to see someone

In short: see a dentist or doctor about any mouth ulcer that lasts more than two weeks, is larger than about a centimetre, comes in large numbers, arrives with fever or feeling unwell, stops you eating or drinking, or keeps coming back more than a few times a year. If a sore comes with difficulty breathing or swelling of the face or throat, that is an emergency; seek urgent care.
