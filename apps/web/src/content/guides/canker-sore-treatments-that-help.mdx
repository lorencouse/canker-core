There is no cure for canker sores, and most heal on their own in one to two weeks whatever you do. What treatment can do is make the days in between more comfortable, protect the sore from further irritation, and in some cases shorten the course a little. This guide covers the options most people try, roughly in order of how easy they are to get. It does not include doses or schedules; follow the product label or the advice of the person who prescribed it.

## Rinses

**Salt-water rinse.** A warm, mild salt-water rinse is the oldest remedy and still a sensible first step. It is cheap, safe to repeat through the day, and helps keep the sore clean. It does not numb the pain and can sting briefly.

**Baking-soda rinse.** Sodium bicarbonate dissolved in water is a gentle alternative that some people find more soothing than salt, and it may help neutralise acidity after meals. Either rinse is fine; the point is to keep the area clean without harsh ingredients.

**Alcohol-free antiseptic mouthwash.** Some over-the-counter mouthwashes are marketed for mouth ulcers. Choose one without alcohol, which stings and dries the tissue.

## Numbing gels and liquids

**Benzocaine** and similar local anaesthetics are sold as gels, liquids and sprays for mouth pain. Applied directly to the sore, they numb it for a short time, which can make eating or brushing possible. They treat the pain, not the sore. Use them as the label directs, and be aware that benzocaine products are not recommended for young children; check age guidance on the pack. If you have ever reacted badly to a local anaesthetic, ask a pharmacist first.

## Protective coatings and patches

Products that form a film over the ulcer, sometimes sold as mouth ulcer patches, pastes or gels containing ingredients such as hyaluronic acid or carboxymethylcellulose, work by shielding the sore from food, teeth and tongue. Many people find that the barrier alone reduces pain noticeably, and keeping the surface undisturbed may help it heal. They can be fiddly to apply and do not stay on forever, but they are worth a try, especially for sores in spots that get knocked a lot.

## Prescription options a dentist may suggest

If your sores are large, frequent or very painful, a dentist or doctor has more to offer:

- **Topical corticosteroid paste or rinse.** A steroid applied to the sore reduces inflammation and, when started early, can shorten healing time and pain. This is the most common prescription treatment for recurrent aphthous ulcers.
- **Chlorhexidine mouthwash.** An antiseptic rinse that may reduce the number of ulcer days and help prevent secondary infection. It can stain teeth with prolonged use, so it is usually a short course.
- **Other options for severe or persistent cases.** These can include stronger anti-inflammatory rinses, a check for nutritional deficiencies with supplementation if one is found, and, rarely, systemic medicines managed by a specialist.

None of these are things to try on your own; they need a diagnosis and instructions from a professional.

## What to avoid while a sore is healing

- **Acidic, spicy, salty and very hot foods and drinks.** They do not necessarily cause the sore, but they will make it hurt more and can keep the surface raw.
- **Sharp or abrasive foods** such as crisps, crusty bread and hard sweets.
- **Alcohol-based mouthwash** and, if you suspect it, toothpaste containing sodium lauryl sulfate.
- **Brushing straight over the sore.** Use a soft brush and go around it.
- **Picking at or repeatedly touching the sore** with your tongue or fingers.
- **Aspirin placed directly on the sore.** This is an old home remedy that can burn the tissue and make things worse.

## How to know what works for you

## When to see someone

Make an appointment with a dentist or doctor if:

- a sore has not started to improve after ten days, or has not healed after two weeks;
- the pain is severe enough to stop you eating or drinking normally;
- you are getting sores so often that comfort measures are not enough;
- a sore is unusually large or deep, or you have many at once;
- you have a fever, feel unwell, or notice sores elsewhere on the body.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, giving a treatment to a child, or taking other medicines, ask a pharmacist before using any over-the-counter product.
